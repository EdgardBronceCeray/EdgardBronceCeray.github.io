Why Gaboriau

The Man Who Invented the Genre

Émile Gaboriau (1832–1873) wrote the first true detective novels: methodical investigation, physical evidence, deduction as drama. His père Tabaret and Monsieur Lecoq were reconstructing crimes from footprints and ash two decades before Sherlock Holmes struck a match on Baker Street — and Conan Doyle knew it well enough to have Holmes sneer at Lecoq by name in A Study in Scarlet. You do not mock what you have not read.

Yet the English-speaking world has largely known Gaboriau through translations made in the nineteenth century — abridged, hurried, and showing their age. The Gaboriau Project exists to change that: new, complete, modern English translations of the man who started it all, beginning where the genre itself began.

The Author

Who Was Émile Gaboriau?

Born in Saujon in 1832, Gaboriau came to Paris, worked as secretary to the feuilleton king Paul Féval, and learned the serial trade from the inside. With L'Affaire Lerouge he invented something new — the roman judiciaire, the novel of investigation — and with Monsieur Lecoq he created the first great professional detective of fiction.

He wrote at a furious pace and died at forty, in 1873, leaving behind the blueprint that Conan Doyle, Agatha Christie and every procedural since would build upon. The genre remembers Baker Street; it began in Paris.

The Translator

Edgard Bronce-Ceray

Edgard Bronce-Ceray is a French-English author, playwright, and literary translator — creator of The Panache Trilogy (Rostand's Cyrano de Bergerac in English alexandrines) and of The Zévaco Project, the first attempt ever made to translate Michel Zévaco's complete novels into English, with twenty-seven volumes published. The Gaboriau Project applies the same method to the inventor of the detective novel.
