The Manifesto

Ending a Century of Silence

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918) was the great rival of Alexandre Dumas in the hearts of French readers: an anarchist journalist turned master of the swashbuckler, whose cloak-and-dagger serials gripped Belle Époque France. Jean-Paul Sartre hailed him as a writer of genius; his novels have been read by millions in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. Yet for over a century, they have remained almost entirely unavailable in English.

The Zévaco Project exists to end that silence. Its goal is simple and unprecedented: every novel Michel Zévaco published, translated into English, one by one, until the canon is complete. No publisher, in more than a hundred years, has attempted it.

For readers of Alexandre Dumas, Rafael Sabatini, Paul Féval and Arturo Pérez-Reverte: historical adventure in the grand French tradition — swordfights, secret passages, doomed loves, poisoners, jesters and kings — in English at last.

The Author

Who Was Michel Zévaco?

Born in Ajaccio in 1860, Michel Zévaco was a schoolteacher, a soldier, and then an anarchist journalist in the Paris of the 1890s — jailed more than once for what he wrote and said. When he turned to fiction, he brought the same fire to the feuilleton: his cloak-and-dagger serials ran in the great Paris dailies and made him, in the hearts of French readers, the one true rival of Alexandre Dumas.

His heroes duel tyrants, mock inquisitors, and rescue the condemned — an anarchist's dream in doublet and hose. Jean-Paul Sartre, who devoured Les Pardaillan as a boy, hailed him as a writer of genius. Zévaco died in 1918; his novels went on to be read by millions in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish — while English readers waited more than a century.
